Why the file is bigger than it needs to be

Open a 20 MB PDF and a 40 KB PDF side by side. Both show the same three pages. The pages look identical. So where's the difference?

Nine times out of ten it's the images. A page of typed text is tiny — under 10 KB compressed — because text is stored as glyph references and font data, not pixels. A scan of that same page is 300 DPI worth of pixels, and at JPEG quality 85 that's maybe 400 KB per page. Fifty pages of that and you have a 20 MB file.

The other one time in ten, someone embedded a high-resolution logo on every single page. Or the PDF has an image that was never compressed at all because it was pasted straight from Photoshop. Or it's a print-ready document with layered vector art that hasn't been flattened. Those exist, they're just less common.

What actually happens when you click "reduce file size"

Adobe's "Reduce File Size" works the way you'd hope. It walks the PDF's object tree, finds every embedded image, decodes it, re-encodes at lower quality, and substitutes it back in. Text, fonts, links, form fields all stay intact. If you have Acrobat Pro, use that — it's the right tool.

The problem is you probably don't have Acrobat Pro. Free viewers on Windows and macOS mostly don't ship that feature, or they hide it somewhere you can't find, or they quietly re-save the file without compressing anything.

Browser-based tools have their own problem. The most popular JavaScript PDF library, pdf-lib, doesn't recompress images — its documentation is upfront about that. It can shuffle the file's internal structure around and save a little space that way, but a scanned PDF that came in at 8 MB goes out at roughly 8 MB. If your portal wants 200 KB, that isn't going to help.

So what does work in a browser? Rendering. Each page gets drawn to a canvas at a chosen DPI, encoded as a JPEG, and assembled into a new PDF. Once every page is a single image, its size is completely under your control. Drop the JPEG quality a little, drop the DPI a little, and 200 KB is trivial.

The cost is real. Text isn't text anymore — it's pixels that look like text. You can't select it, you can't search it, you can't hand it to a screen reader. Your portal doesn't care about any of this, but if you're keeping a copy of a signed contract for yourself, this matters.

That's why there are two modes here. Rasterize hits the target. Keep-text does the structural trick and admits up front it probably won't reach 200 KB.

Picking a resolution

96 DPI renders the page at 1.33 pixels per point. A Letter page (612×792 points) becomes 816×1056 pixels. That's screen-readable and produces reasonable file sizes for a portal upload.

72 DPI is roughly 1:1 with the PDF's own coordinate system. Smallest files, softest text. Use it when the target is very tight and the document is a scan you only need to be able to identify.

120 DPI is what I'd choose for anything that might get printed. Text at 120 looks noticeably sharper than 96, without the file size of 150 or 200.

150 DPI is worth the extra bytes when the reviewer might actually print and hold the page — a certificate, a stamped form, an ID card.

Dropping pages is the cheapest win

If a form asks for "the signed page" it doesn't want the other forty pages of boilerplate. If a visa application wants your passport bio page, it doesn't want twenty-three other pages of stamps and old visas. Most portals don't require the whole document — just the specific pages they've asked for.

Unchecking pages before compressing is the easiest and biggest size saving available. A 40-page scan uncompressed is 40 MB. Ten pages of it at 96 DPI is 200 KB. That's not a compression trick, that's just not sending bytes the recipient doesn't need.

Use the include/exclude checkboxes above. Order is preserved — the output keeps the pages you kept, in their original sequence.

When 200 KB is impossible

Some PDFs have a floor you can't get below. A 200-page document full of photographs won't fit in 200 KB at any quality that's still legible. Neither will a scan at 600 DPI that the scanner saved in a lossless format — the tool can re-encode it aggressively, but the pixels are already there.

When that happens the tool tells you. It reports the smallest result it achieved, shows the exact DPI and quality it used, and stops there. Three things you can do next: split the PDF into pieces (portals usually accept multiple uploads), drop pages you don't strictly need, or check whether the portal actually allows a larger file than the number on the form suggests — a surprising number have soft limits.

Sizes

How do I compress a PDF to 100 KB?

Set the target to 100 KB, keep rasterize mode selected, and hit Compress. Scans usually hit 100 KB at 96 DPI without looking much worse. Text-only PDFs are usually already under 100 KB — if yours is bigger, there are images in it and the tool will find and recompress them. If 96 DPI can't reach 100 KB, the tool drops to 84, then 72, then 60, then 48 DPI before giving up.

What does 200 KB give me that 100 KB doesn't?

Roughly double the quality budget. At 200 KB the tool usually lands at 96 DPI with JPEG quality around 0.5–0.7, which is legible on screen and printable at a pinch. At 100 KB it's often 0.3–0.4, which starts to show as blockiness on photographic scans. If your portal allows 200 KB, use it.

Why did I ask for 200 KB and get 140 KB?

Because the binary search stops at the first quality setting that fits under 200. If quality 0.6 gives 140 KB and quality 0.62 gives 210 KB, it stays at 0.6. The 60 KB of unused budget isn't wasted — the image just didn't need it. If you want the file closer to the ceiling, raise the DPI or use rasterize's higher-quality end.

Can I compress a PDF to 500 KB or 1 MB?

Easily. At 500 KB the tool usually stops at 120 DPI with quality around 0.8, which is close to the original. At 1 MB it's often 150 DPI with quality 0.85. For anything the recipient might print, these are the targets to aim for.

Portals

Why does a government portal reject my PDF for size?

Because they enforce hard caps. Government, visa, and exam portals receive millions of uploads and can't store them at whatever size people send. They publish a ceiling — 100 KB, 200 KB, 500 KB, 1 MB, sometimes 2 MB — and reject anything over. Most don't downscale; they just fail the upload.

What size limits do common portals actually use?

Indian government exams (SSC, UPSC, IBPS) typically want the full application PDF under 200–500 KB, and photo/signature as separate files with their own caps. US DS-160 confirmation pages allow 2 MB. Schengen visa document uploads are usually 1 MB per file. Most job portals accept resumes between 500 KB and 5 MB. The number on the form is what counts — always check it.

Will the compressed PDF still open in the portal's viewer?

Yes. The output is a standard PDF 1.7. Some very old portal viewers might show very low-DPI scans as slightly soft, but the file structure is normal and any viewer that could open the original can open the compressed version.

My portal asks for a minimum DPI. Does this still work?

Yes — pick that DPI from the dropdown and the tool will keep it. If the portal demands 150 DPI and a 200 KB cap, and your scan can't fit both, the tool reports the smallest file it could produce at 150 DPI rather than silently dropping to a lower DPI to force a fit.

Text & quality

Does compressing make the text unselectable?

In rasterize mode, yes. Every page becomes an image, so there's no text left to select. In keep-text mode the text stays selectable, but you'll get 5–20% savings at best, and often zero on files that are already structurally tidy.

What about forms, checkboxes, and hyperlinks?

All flattened in rasterize mode. A fillable form becomes a picture of a form. A checkbox becomes a picture of a checkbox. If the PDF has interactive elements you still need to use, fill them out first, print-to-PDF from a viewer to flatten the form fields into the page, and then compress the signed version.

Will it look OK when printed?

At 96 DPI it prints legibly but looks slightly soft, especially on small print. At 120 DPI it's fine for most purposes. At 150 DPI it's hard to distinguish from a normal photocopy. At 72 DPI it looks poor on paper — 72 is a screen-only setting.

Can I compress a scanned PDF without losing legibility?

Yes, if you don't compress too hard. Scans are already images, so the tool is essentially re-encoding them at a lower quality. A 300 DPI scan at 150 DPI is usually perfectly readable. At 72 DPI it starts to look fuzzy, especially on handwriting and stamps. Choose your DPI based on what's actually on the page — printed documents survive compression much better than handwritten notes.

Pages

Which pages should I drop?

Whatever the form doesn't ask for. An exam application form usually needs the declaration page with the signature, not the 30 pages of instructions. A visa upload needs the passport bio page and possibly the photo page — not the whole 24-page passport. A court filing needs only the pages being filed, not the case history. Every page you drop saves its share of the bytes.

Does unchecking a page reorder the rest?

No. The output keeps the pages you left checked, in their original sequence. If you need to reorder, use a PDF editor first and bring the reordered file back here.

Can I preview a page before deciding?

Yes — click any thumbnail to see it at a larger size in the Preview panel. The preview shows what the page looks like when rendered at the currently selected DPI. If it looks legible there, that's what your portal will see.

Failures

What if it can't reach my target?

The tool reports the smallest result it produced, shows the DPI and quality it used, and stops. It won't hand you a file that's over your target and call it done. Your options at that point: split the PDF into pieces, drop more pages, or check whether the portal really does reject at the number on the form — many have soft limits that accept a bit more.

Why does a 40-page scan take so long?

Every page is rendered and JPEG-encoded multiple times during the binary search. A 40-page document with a tight target might mean 40 pages × 3 passes = 120 render operations. On a fast machine that's a couple of minutes. On an older laptop it can be five. The progress bar shows which page it's currently rendering so you can tell it hasn't frozen.

Compression failed with "encrypted". What now?

The PDF has a password or a permissions password set. pdf.js won't render password-protected files without the password. Open the PDF in a viewer, use "Print to PDF" to save a new copy — that usually strips the encryption — and bring the new copy back here.

What if the output PDF is corrupted?

It shouldn't be — pdf-lib produces well-formed PDF 1.7 output. If your portal rejects the file it's almost certainly for size or for a feature the portal doesn't accept (some portal validators reject any PDF that has embedded JPEGs above a certain resolution, even when the byte count is fine). Try a lower DPI in that case, or check the portal's technical requirements page.

Privacy

Does my PDF get uploaded anywhere?

No. The file is read from your disk, rendered, and reassembled entirely inside your browser tab. The only network requests this page makes are for the two libraries it needs — pdf.js and pdf-lib — and neither of those receives your document.

Can I use this offline?

After the libraries have loaded once, yes. Both are cached by the browser. You can disconnect from the network and continue compressing documents in the same session.

Which libraries does it use, and are they safe?

pdf.js is Mozilla's PDF renderer, used inside Firefox. pdf-lib is a well-known MIT-licensed PDF writer. Both are heavily used, actively maintained, and neither sends data anywhere. If you want to be certain, check your browser's network tab while compressing — the only requests will be for the library files themselves.

Is there a watermark, or a hidden file limit?

No watermark, no signup, no daily cap, no subscription. The output PDF has nothing appended. The 100 MB upload limit is a memory guard, not a business limit.
