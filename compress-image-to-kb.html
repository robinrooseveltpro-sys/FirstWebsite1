How to compress an image to an exact file size

Most image compressors give you a quality slider — you pick 80%, wait, check the file size, adjust to 70%, check again, and repeat until the file fits. That is open-loop: you are the feedback controller. This tool is closed-loop: you give it the target size, and it finds the quality automatically.

The underlying algorithm is a binary search over the JPEG or WebP quality parameter. Quality ranges from 0 to 1, but the relationship between quality and file size is not linear — the size curve is steep at the top and flattens toward zero. A linear scan wastes encodings; a binary search converges in about eight to ten attempts:

  1. Encode at the maximum quality (0.92). If the result is under your target, use it — the image is already as good as it can be.
  2. Encode at the minimum quality (0.05). If that's still over your target, the target is unreachable at these dimensions — the tool either downscales or reports the constraint.
  3. Otherwise, binary search between 0.05 and 0.92. Each iteration halves the interval: 0.485 → 0.75 → 0.9 → 0.82 → … The search stops when the interval is under 2 percentage points.
  4. Polish pass: try one more encode slightly above the last success, in case the discrete quality step left a few percent on the table.

The result is the highest quality that fits under your target, with a few percent margin so the file is genuinely usable. The information panel shows the exact byte count, the final quality, and the number of iterations used — because you deserve to know what happened.

Why a fixed target beats a quality slider

For most people, the goal is not "as small as possible" — it is fit under a hard cap. Exam portals require exactly this. So do job applications, email attachments, and dozens of online forms:

Why JPEG and WebP — not PNG

PNG is a lossless format, which means it has no quality parameter to binary-search over. Its only lever is dimensions and colour palette. For "compress to X KB", PNG is the wrong format — you either hit the target by downscaling (which changes the image) or you accept whatever size PNG produces.

JPEG and WebP both expose a quality parameter, which is what makes binary search possible. WebP typically produces files about 25% smaller than JPEG at the same perceptual quality, but JPEG is more compatible with older portals. This tool supports both.

Auto mode vs exact mode

Auto mode preserves the original photo's aspect ratio, caps the working dimensions at 3000 pixels on the long edge, and downscales progressively (0.8× per step) if the target is unreachable at full size. This is the right choice for "my upload was rejected for being too large" — you want the photo to look the same, just smaller in bytes.

Exact mode forces the output to specific pixel dimensions — essential for exams that require 200×230 or 140×60. When the source aspect ratio does not match, you choose how to reconcile them:

  • Crop to fill — center the image and trim the overflow. Best for portraits, where the face is usually central.
  • Fit within — letterbox the image with a white border. Preserves the whole photo at the cost of white space.
  • Stretch — distort to exactly fill the frame. Almost never the right choice, but sometimes portals demand it.

What happens when the target is unreachable

Two failure cases are handled explicitly, not hidden:

  • Already under target at max quality. The source image at these dimensions is smaller than your target even at quality 0.92. The tool returns it at max quality and reports the fact. To use more of your budget, increase the dimensions.
  • Still over target at min quality. The source at these dimensions produces a file larger than your target even at quality 0.05. In auto mode, the tool downscales and retries. In exact mode, it reports the constraint honestly — the target is not reachable at those pixel dimensions without changing them.

Privacy

All encoding happens in your browser using the Canvas API. The photo is never uploaded to a server. You can verify this in your browser's network tab: there is no request carrying the image. The tool also works offline after the page loads.

Frequently asked questions

How do I compress an image to exactly 50 KB?

Set the target to 50 KB, upload your photo, and click Compress to target. The tool binary-searches the JPEG quality until the encoded file lands just under 50 KB, then reports the exact size and the quality it used.

Does this tool upload my photo?

No. Everything runs in your browser. Nothing is sent to a server, and the tool works offline after the page loads.

Why does it produce a size slightly under my target?

The target is a maximum, not an exact match. The tool finds the highest quality that fits under your target, so the result is always at or below the number you entered. It converges to within about 2% of the target, so the file is usually within a few percent of the limit.

What is the difference between auto mode and exact mode?

Auto mode preserves the photo's aspect ratio, caps the working size at 3000 pixels, and downscales progressively if the target is unreachable. Exact mode forces the output to specific pixel dimensions — required for exam photos and signatures.

Why does the tool sometimes report "already under target at max quality"?

That means your photo, at the working dimensions, is smaller than your target even at quality 0.92. You are already using less than your file-size budget. To use more of it, increase the dimensions or reduce the target and try again.

What happens if the target is impossible at the chosen dimensions?

In auto mode, the tool downscales the image and retries. In exact mode, it reports the constraint and the best size it achieved, so you can decide whether to change the target or the dimensions.

Does this work for exam photos and signatures?

Is this tool really free, with no watermark and no subscription?
