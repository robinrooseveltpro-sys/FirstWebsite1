How this tool turns a photo into a coloring page

The pipeline is a classical computer-vision technique, not an AI model. Every step runs in your browser using the Canvas API:

  1. Flatten and cap resolution. The photo is scaled to at most 2000 pixels on the long edge (so it stays fast) and any transparency is flattened to white.
  2. Grayscale. Colour is discarded by weighting channels — 0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 B — which matches how the human eye perceives brightness.
  3. Gaussian blur. A small separable blur smooths JPEG noise and film grain before edge detection, so speckles do not become lines.
  4. Sobel edge detection. For each pixel, a 3×3 Sobel operator estimates the gradient magnitude. Pixels above a threshold become black (lines); the rest become white (paper).
  5. Dilation. The binary mask is grown by 0–4 iterations to produce thicker lines. This is the “line thickness” slider.

The detail slider controls the Sobel threshold. A lower detail setting raises the threshold (only the strongest edges survive); a higher detail setting lowers it (more edges survive).

How color-by-number mode works

On top of the same edge pipeline, color-by-number adds a quantization step:

  1. K-means colour quantization. The photo's colours are reduced to N representative colours (the slider). K-means iteratively clusters pixel colours into K groups and returns the centroid of each group as the palette.
  2. Region detection. Each pixel gets assigned to its nearest palette colour, then a scanline flood fill finds connected regions of the same colour.
  3. Numbering. Regions larger than a minimum area get a number placed at their centroid, indicating which palette colour they should be filled with.
  4. Legend. A palette legend at the bottom of the canvas maps each number to its colour and hex value.

The output is a proper printable color-by-number sheet, not just a colorized version of the photo.

Getting the best results

  • Choose photos with clear subjects. A single face, pet, or object against a simple background produces the cleanest line art. Busy scenes become noisy.
  • Use the noise reduction slider for phone photos. Grain from low light or heavy compression will otherwise become speckled noise in the edges.
  • Increase line thickness for kids. Thicker lines (3–4) are easier to colour between.
  • Lower detail for a cleaner page. If the output looks cluttered, drop the detail slider from 5 to 3 or 2.
  • Use invert for dark photos. If the source is mostly dark, the default output will be mostly white noise. Invert gives white lines on black.

Resolution and print quality

The tool processes images at up to 2000 pixels on the longest side. At 300 dpi that prints at about 6.7 inches on the long edge, which is fine for a kids’ colouring page. At 200 dpi it prints at 10 inches, closer to A4 proportions. If your original photo is larger, it will be downscaled to 2000 px before processing, so the download will match that resolution.

Limitations — read before relying on the output

  • This is a classical edge-detection tool, not an AI model. It produces technical line art from the edges it finds. It does not stylise, colour, or reconstruct like a generative model would.
  • Busy photos produce noisy line art. A crowded scene with lots of texture will have many small edges. Use the detail slider to filter them out.
  • Very small faces or objects may produce too few edges to form a recognizable outline.
  • The color-by-number region numbering only labels regions above a minimum area (currently 80 pixels). Tiny fragments are left unlabelled, which is intentional — otherwise the page becomes unreadable.
  • The colour palette is quantized, so gradients and subtle shading will flatten into a small set of solid colours. This is the point of color-by-number, but it means the result looks nothing like a photograph.
  • No vector output. The result is a raster PNG. If you need SVG line art, trace it in a vector tool after download.

Privacy — why this runs in the browser

Every step — grayscale, blur, edge detection, dilation, quantization, flood fill, and PNG generation — happens locally using JavaScript. The photo is never sent to a server, and the tool keeps working offline after the page loads. You can verify this in your browser’s network tab: there is no request carrying the image.

Frequently asked questions

How do I turn a photo into a coloring page?

Upload a photo, choose the coloring page mode, and adjust the line thickness and detail sliders. The tool runs edge detection in your browser and produces a printable black-and-white line drawing. Click Download PNG when it looks right.

Does this tool upload my photo?

No. The photo is processed entirely in your browser. Nothing is sent to a server, and the tool works offline after the page loads.

How is color-by-number different from coloring page mode?

Coloring page mode produces only the line art. Color-by-number mode also quantizes the photo into a small palette of solid colors, labels each color region with a number, and adds a printable legend so you can color it in.

Why does the result look noisy?

Edge detection finds every edge, including texture and film grain. Use the noise reduction slider (higher values) and lower the detail slider to filter out weak edges. Clear photos with simple backgrounds produce the cleanest results.

What size will the download be?

The result is downloaded as a PNG at up to 2000 pixels on the long edge. At 300 dpi that prints at roughly 6.7 inches; at 200 dpi it prints at roughly 10 inches.

Can I use this for commercial purposes?

The tool itself imposes no restriction on the output. However, you are responsible for ensuring you have the rights to any photo you process. Do not upload photos you do not own or have permission to use.

Does this work on my phone?

Yes. The tool is responsive and works on phones and tablets. Very large photos may take a few seconds to process on older devices.

What if my photo is very dark?

Enable the Invert option to get white lines on a black background. Or brighten the original photo in an image editor first — the tool processes whatever brightness you give it.

Is my photo stored anywhere?

No. The photo exists only in the page’s memory for the current session. Nothing is written to localStorage, cookies, or a server.
